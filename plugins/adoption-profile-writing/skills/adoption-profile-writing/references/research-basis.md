# Why These Rules

The evidence behind the language rules in `SKILL.md`. Read it when a rule looks wrong, or when someone asks why profiles are written this way.

Five primary studies, two guides for the rules the studies do not cover, and the industry checklist the rules were checked against:

- **Markowitz (2020)**, *Journal of Applied Social Psychology* 50(3), 160–173, https://doi.org/10.1111/jasp.12647. Study 1 tracked how long 184,805 Petfinder profiles stayed online. Study 2 compared 676,004 profiles of adopted and unadopted pets. Study 3 was an experiment with 987 readers. The author describes the effect sizes as small.
- **Kelling et al. (2026)**, *Society & Animals* 34(2), 179–199, https://doi.org/10.1163/15685306-bja10192, published online 2024. 561 readers rated mock dog adoption ads, with "plain" and "cute" wordings of the same behavioral challenge. It measured what readers understood and said they wanted, not adoptions, so it informs wording and does not set how much of a card goes to struggles.
- **Nakamura et al. (2019)**, *Animals* 9(7), 464, https://doi.org/10.3390/ani9070464. Matched 45 personality words in 70,733 PetRescue (Australia) dog listings, 2004 to 2013, against days listed, a stand-in for length of stay. Outcome data, correlational, with breed-specific effects.
- **Rix et al. (2021)**, *Animals* 11(1), 62, https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11010062. Matched the narrative voice (first or third person) and the name category of 4,460 adult cats rehomed from three UK shelters, 2011 to 2016, against length of stay, with age and sex in the model. Outcome data, retrospective; who chose the voice is unrecorded.
- **Archer, Hall and Andrukonis (2025)**, *Animal Welfare* 34, e75, https://doi.org/10.1017/awf.2025.10043. 1,171 readers (103 at a US municipal shelter, the rest online) rated 40 dog photos shown with and without a visually assigned breed label and with one of nine marketing descriptions or none. It measured stated likelihood to adopt, not adoptions, and each description was a trait word heading two sentences of behavior.
- **Humane World for Animals, *Adopters Welcome* (2025)**, https://humanepro.org/sites/default/files/documents/guides-adopters-welcome-2025-final-digital.pdf. The source for the rule against conditions on the adopter.
- **Best Friends Animal Society, "Animal Shelter Dogs: Promoting Them for Adoption"**, https://bestfriends.org/network/resources-tools/animal-shelter-dogs-promoting-them-adoption. The source for the opening and closing rules. See the second-to-last section below.
- **Shelter Animals Count, *Checklist for Improving Adoptions in Animal Shelters and Rescues* (2026)**, https://www.shelteranimalscount.org/adoptions-checklist/. Thirty-two research-cited practices, eight of them on online profiles, citing Archer, Rix, Kelling, and Nakamura among fourteen references. The rules were checked against those eight on 2026-10-29. See the last section below.

## What increases adoption interest

**Analytic, fact-based writing.** In Study 2, adopted pets' profiles used more analytic language, measured by articles ("a," "the") and prepositions, than unadopted pets' profiles. Markowitz counts pronouns, adverbs, conjunctions, and negations as storytelling words, the opposite pole. In Study 3, readers of an analytic, low-social profile said they were more likely to adopt the pet (Cohen's d = .18) and to visit the shelter (d = .16) than readers of a storytelling, high-social profile.

The Study 3 contrast bundled two things: the storytelling profile was also the high-social one. No study here separates emotional and social language from a factual account told as a connected scene, so `SKILL.md`'s rule to tell observations as scenes is an editorial decision, made because profiles written to the analytic measure read as dry checklists. It keeps everything the evidence does support: facts over emotional appeals, behavior over labels, no social words. A scene does use more pronouns and conjunctions, which Markowitz counts toward the storytelling pole, so tracking a shelter's own days-to-placement before and after the change is how to tell whether it costs anything.

**Third person.** Rix matched 4,460 cat profiles, 40% written as the cat ("Hi my name is Bear, I am a sweet boy and I would love my own home") and 60% about it ("Tiger is a handsome chap who came to us as a stray"), against days to rehoming. First-person cats stayed a mean 21.6 days and third-person cats 19.1, with age and sex in the model (p < 0.001), and the survival curves separated (log-rank p = 0.003). The authors read it through Markowitz: a third-person narrator reads as informational and objective, a first-person one as intimate, and adopters behave as consumers of an advertisement rather than as readers of a story. The study is on cats and retrospective, and the authors raise the possibility that staff wrote harder cats in the first person, which the data cannot exclude. It is the only outcome study of voice, so `SKILL.md` keeps every card in the third person and the checker fails first-person pronouns. The cat's name made no difference to length of stay, so a rename for adoptability buys nothing.

**Behavior over labels, including the kind ones.** Nakamura's listings stayed longer when they used a trait label, positive or negative: "friendly" 1.14 times as long as listings without it, "gentle" 1.14, "quiet" 1.15, "intelligent" 1.13, "obedient" 1.24, "trainable" 1.25, "energetic" 1.22. A few upbeat words went the other way: "lively" 0.88, "eager" 0.92, "independent" 0.73, "make you proud" 0.36 (306 listings). The authors flag that a large sample makes small differences significant, that some words were rare ("restless," 37 listings), and that effects varied by breed ("energetic" ran 1.81 times as long for cattle dogs and 1.09 for Labradors). The direction still matches Markowitz from a different country and dataset: describe the behavior, not the trait. Archer read the other way at first sight: readers rated a photo higher with any of eight positive descriptions than with no text, "calm" (63.8 of 100) and "affectionate" (62.5) highest against 51.7 for the photo alone. But each description was a trait word heading two sentences of behavior ("He loves to cuddle and sleep with you. This dog patiently waits for you to return home so he can get all the belly rubs"), set against no description at all, so the study cannot separate the word from the sentences, and it measured stated interest rather than days listed. The two agree where they overlap: "energetic" (48.6, the one description rated below no text) and "lively" and "eager" (rated well, and shorter stays in Nakamura). So a positive word may head the behavior that shows it ("calm: he sleeps through the kennel noise and settles on his bed within minutes of coming in") and may not stand in for it, and "energetic," "active," and "high energy" stay out in either position.

**Struggles as behaviors.** Nakamura's negative trait words carried the largest penalties among the labels: "dominant" 1.46, "sensitive" 1.42, "hyperactive" 1.32, "timid" 1.20, "submissive" 1.16. The study cannot separate the word from the dog it describes, so read this as "the trait label is the costliest way to report a struggle," not "the word causes the stay." Listings that named a trait to deny it ("not dominant," 24 listings; "not hyperactive," 34) also ran long. `SKILL.md` writes each struggle as a behavior a reader can picture and never names a trait to deny it. Archer's "Non-Dominant" description rated second highest of nine (63.6), but it came with "easily trained and loves to follow directions" and "loves all other dogs," which is the behavior the rule says to write in the denial's place, and the study cannot separate the two. Neither study isolates the denial, so the rule rests on the mechanism: the denial plants the question it answers.

**Placement constraints.** "Only dog" ran 1.57 times as long and "no cats" 1.37. The constraint is a fact about the dog and is published as a search filter either way, so `SKILL.md` keeps it; the finding marks constrained dogs as the ones that need the most promotion, not the ones whose sentence should go.

**Positive, creative wording within these limits.** Markowitz's findings rule out emotional appeals, superlatives, and social words. They do not rule out a lively, positive account of observed behavior: no study here tests humor, a nickname, or vivid verbs on their own. `SKILL.md` allows them when each rests on something the dog does (an editorial decision).

**Brevity.** In Study 1, word count was positively associated with days online (β = .03): longer profiles stayed up longer. The effect is small. `SKILL.md` still holds profiles to 175-250 words and penalizes going more than 20% past that; the steepness is an editorial decision rather than a measured effect, and the rule against trading a disclosure for length stands.

**Photos, with mixed evidence.** In Study 2, adopted pets' profiles carried more photos than unadopted pets' profiles. In Study 1, the number of photos was positively associated with days online (β = .04). The study does not settle which way photos push, so the photo target in `SKILL.md` rests on Study 2 and on Markowitz's argument that photos reduce an online reader's uncertainty, not on a measured effect. Archer puts the picture above the text: with no label or description, the photo alone explained 6% of the variance in rated adoptability against 2% for every text effect together, and the reader explained 44%, so a dog one reader passes over another rates highly. The count is what a writer can change; the shot is where the gain is.

## What deters interest

**Social and humanizing words.** In Study 1, profiles with higher rates of social words stayed online longer (β = .05), and in Study 2, low rates of social words best predicted that a profile belonged to an adopted pet. Markowitz's examples of social words include "boy," "girl," "friend," "soulmate," and "sweetheart." The papers measure the association and do not test why it occurs.

**The breed label.** Archer's readers rated the same photos 1.9 points lower out of 100 with a breed label than without (54.1 against 56.0, p = 0.002). Chihuahua, Chow, Jack Russell Terrier, Miniature Pinscher, and Terrier mix labels lowered the rating; "Lab mix" raised it; the pit bull label made no difference, against most earlier work. Cohen et al. (2020), cited there, saw median length of stay fall from 30.3 to 19 days when three New York shelters took breed labels off their adoption cards, with no rise in returns. The label on the listing is a shelter-system attribute that staff set; `SKILL.md` keeps the breed out of the copy, where it repeats the panel and adds nothing.

**Conditions on the adopter.** *Adopters Welcome* says requirements such as fenced yards "significantly decrease the pool of adopters." Neither study tests adopter conditions in a listing.

The skill applies this to conditions on the *adopter*, which is where the mechanism operates: an elimination question shrinks the applicant pool while telling nobody anything about the animal. It does not apply to constraints that are true of the animal, and it does not override the one-struggle floor. See "Placement constraints stay" in `SKILL.md`.

**Euphemism for why the animal is at the shelter.** Kelling tested "her last owners surrendered her" against "her last owners could not meet all her needs," and the two wordings changed how readers explained the surrender. 45 participants volunteered that they wanted to know why the dog was at the shelter.

**Promises in place of observations.** *Adopters Welcome* asks counselors to be "transparent with facts and observations, while avoiding promises." McGuire et al. (2020), *Animals* 10(9), 1702, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7552731/, found that dogs a shelter evaluation flagged for resource guarding guarded in their adoptive homes 47.4% of the time, so a behavior seen at the shelter is worth reporting and a forecast built on it often fails. Archer's "friendly" description promised "well behaved around others and loves to play with small children" and raised rated interest, and the authors name the cost in their limitations: adopters with higher expectations for their dog's behavior returned it more often (Powell et al. 2022a) and adopted again less often (Powell et al. 2022b). Interest and retention part on a promise, and the placements that count are the ones that are kept.

## The takeaway

Profiles of adopted pets use more analytic language and fewer social words than profiles of unadopted pets, and readers in an experiment preferred the analytic version. The effects are small and correlational at scale, and the experiment measured stated intent rather than adoptions. That is enough to rule out emotional appeals and social words, and it is why `SKILL.md` says to follow the rules when instinct prefers emotional copy. It does not rule out telling observed behavior as a warm, lively scene. Rix adds the narrator: cats described in the third person went home sooner than cats who introduced themselves, so the scene is told about the animal, never by it.

## Where the two-struggle cap comes from

`SKILL.md` has every card name one or two light struggles and sends the rest to the pre-meet history review. This is an editorial decision, and no study here measures it in either direction:

- **Markowitz** measured language style (analytic against storytelling, social words), not how many difficulties a profile disclosed.
- **Kelling** measured what readers understood from mock ads and what they said they wanted. Readers ranked behavior first among the information on offer (mean rank 1.97, first for 50.5% of 546 respondents), but the study did not follow any ad to an adoption, so it cannot say whether a card with five struggles places better or worse than one with two.
- **Humane World and Best Friends** both steer the profile toward promotion and put the full behavior history in the adoption conversation.

The cap rests on what one shelter's cards showed. Cards for harder dogs that listed every difficulty in the record read as mostly problems: one draft named five struggles in 64 of 238 words. A card that named two light ones, said where the jumping happens and that it is improving, and read as a dog worth meeting. The one-struggle floor keeps the card honest; the history review carries what the card leaves out.

## Where the serious-incident rule comes from

`SKILL.md` routes five kinds of serious incident to the pre-meet consultation: a bite, an attack on another animal, guarding that made contact, anything involving a child, and a serious injury to a cat or small animal. Each needs its circumstances explained and questions answered, which a person in a consultation can do and a web page cannot.

No study tests whether a bite history belongs in public copy, in either direction, so treat any claim that publishing one changes clicks, applications, or returns as unsupported. The rule rests on peer practice, with one wording point from Kelling:

- **Keeping the incident off the card is the industry norm.** No national body instructs one in the listing. Humane World for Animals and Best Friends both put disclosure in the adoption conversation and steer the profile toward promotion. Some rescues publish bites in the profile body anyway, so a reader who has seen their cards may expect it.
- **A struggle on the card has to stay recognizable.** Readers of Kelling's "cute" ads identified the stated behavioral challenge less often than readers of the plain ads: dog aggression 87.5% of the time from "tends to display aggression towards other dogs" and 52.2% from "is picky about his friends." That is a comprehension finding, not an adoption one, and it is why the one or two struggles that stay on the card carry a concrete example under their gentle framing.
- **Profiles that publish incidents are the counterexample, not the model.** They tend to show the failures the language rules target: clinical vocabulary published raw, adopter screening stacked onto a disclosure, handling prescribed to the adopter, and one incident retold in place of the pattern.

## Where the softened-constraint rule comes from

Best Friends Animal Society's "Animal Shelter Dogs: Promoting Them for Adoption" is the industry's model for adoption bios, and the rules here split from it on one technique while agreeing with it on three others.

**The split.** On constraints it instructs: "You can include restrictions, but be gentle and choose your words carefully. For example, instead of saying 'Andy must go to a home without children,' which could scare off even a childless couple, put a positive spin on it: 'Jo-Jo's such a goofy bundle of energy that he might be a bit too much for small children.'" Strip the charm from that rewrite and no constraint is left standing. The reasoning behind the instruction is about the applicant pool rather than the reader's understanding, and it does not hold here, because shelter-management systems publish the constraint as a structured attribute either way. The softening costs the reader the fact and saves the funnel nothing.

The guide works the same move on behavior. It rejects "Joey unfortunately has leash reactivity, which means he behaves offensively toward other dogs when he's on walks. He can live peaceably with other dogs, as long as they're submissive" in favor of "Joey is that rare sort of pooch whom a child can climb all over and hug. He's so gentle and sweet around everyone. Well, except other dogs when he's out on a walk. He's not so good with other pooches when he's on leash." Two of its three objections to the first version hold here: "leash reactivity" is shelter vocabulary, and "as long as they're submissive" is a condition on the adopter. The third does not. The rewrite moves the behavior behind two clauses of praise and lands on "not so good with other pooches," which names no behavior a reader can picture. Fix the jargon and the adopter condition, keep the plain clause, and add the example: "Barks and pulls toward other dogs he passes on walks."

**The agreement.** Three of its instructions match the rules above and are worth knowing are not idiosyncratic to one shelter: keep the language "simple and conversational; avoid animal welfare jargon or technical terms whenever possible"; open on something specific rather than the list of attributes it gives as the failure case ("Joey is a five-year-old neutered male pittie/Lab mix with white markings, up-to-date on shots"); and close by inviting contact rather than screening for it.


## Where the rules stand against the Shelter Animals Count checklist

Shelter Animals Count's checklist for improving adoptions (page dated 2026-08-12) lists eight online-profile practices, cited to Archer, Rix, Kelling, Nakamura, and ten sources this file does not hold. The rules were checked against it on 2026-10-29.

- **Implemented as written.** Third person (Rix). The breed out of the copy, with behavior where the checklist says personality (Archer). "Energetic" out, which Archer and Nakamura agree on. No condition on the adopter in the profile, which the checklist's health item also recommends.
- **Implemented with a difference.** A positive personality word goes beside the behavior that shows it rather than being added on its own, because Nakamura's bare labels ran long and each of Archer's descriptions bundled the word with two sentences of behavior. The photo target is 3 to 5 where the checklist's own guide says 4 to 7, or 2 with a video; the band here rests on Markowitz Study 2 and the five slides listing sites syndicate.
- **Outside the copy.** A clear adoption process, which the closing lines cover as far as a profile can (the application, the history review, the meet-and-greet) and the rest of which is the shelter's site and its response time. Promotion aimed at the animals likely to wait longest, which is a choice of which profiles to work on first. Breed labels on kennel cards, a shelter-system attribute staff set.
- **No rule yet.** The checklist's guide asks for specific health information (what a condition costs per month, what the shelter covers, the dates of the last dental cleaning and the next vaccines) in place of vague wording. The template's program blocks state what the shelter covers for the animals they apply to; nothing here governs how a health fact is written elsewhere on the card.
