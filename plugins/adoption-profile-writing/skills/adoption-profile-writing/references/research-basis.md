# Why These Rules

The evidence behind the language rules in `SKILL.md`. Read it when a rule looks wrong, or when someone asks why profiles are written this way.

Three primary studies, plus two guides for the rules the studies do not cover:

- **Markowitz (2020)**, *Journal of Applied Social Psychology* 50(3), 160–173, https://doi.org/10.1111/jasp.12647. Study 1 tracked how long 184,805 Petfinder profiles stayed online. Study 2 compared 676,004 profiles of adopted and unadopted pets. Study 3 was an experiment with 987 readers. The author describes the effect sizes as small.
- **Kelling et al. (2026)**, *Society & Animals* 34(2), 179–199, https://doi.org/10.1163/15685306-bja10192, published online 2024. 561 readers rated mock dog adoption ads, with "plain" and "cute" wordings of the same behavioral challenge. It measured what readers understood and said they wanted, not adoptions, so it informs wording and does not set how much of a card goes to struggles.
- **Nakamura et al. (2019)**, *Animals* 9(7), 464, https://doi.org/10.3390/ani9070464. Matched 45 personality words in 70,733 PetRescue (Australia) dog listings, 2004 to 2013, against days listed, a stand-in for length of stay. Outcome data, correlational, with breed-specific effects.
- **Humane World for Animals, *Adopters Welcome* (2025)**, https://humanepro.org/sites/default/files/documents/guides-adopters-welcome-2025-final-digital.pdf. The source for the rule against conditions on the adopter.
- **Best Friends Animal Society, "Animal Shelter Dogs: Promoting Them for Adoption"**, https://bestfriends.org/network/resources-tools/animal-shelter-dogs-promoting-them-adoption. The source for the opening and closing rules. See the last section below.

## What increases adoption interest

**Analytic, fact-based writing.** In Study 2, adopted pets' profiles used more analytic language, measured by articles ("a," "the") and prepositions, than unadopted pets' profiles. Markowitz counts pronouns, adverbs, conjunctions, and negations as storytelling words, the opposite pole. In Study 3, readers of an analytic, low-social profile said they were more likely to adopt the pet (Cohen's d = .18) and to visit the shelter (d = .16) than readers of a storytelling, high-social profile.

The Study 3 contrast bundled two things: the storytelling profile was also the high-social one. No study here separates emotional and social language from a factual account told as a connected scene, so `SKILL.md`'s rule to tell observations as scenes is an editorial decision, made because profiles written to the analytic measure read as dry checklists. It keeps everything the evidence does support: facts over emotional appeals, behavior over labels, no social words. A scene does use more pronouns and conjunctions, which Markowitz counts toward the storytelling pole, so the grader's calibration against days-to-placement is how to tell whether it costs anything.

**Behavior over labels, including the kind ones.** Nakamura's listings stayed longer when they used a trait label, positive or negative: "friendly" 1.14 times as long as listings without it, "gentle" 1.14, "quiet" 1.15, "intelligent" 1.13, "obedient" 1.24, "trainable" 1.25, "energetic" 1.22. A few upbeat words went the other way: "lively" 0.88, "eager" 0.92, "independent" 0.73, "make you proud" 0.36 (306 listings). The authors flag that a large sample makes small differences significant, that some words were rare ("restless," 37 listings), and that effects varied by breed ("energetic" ran 1.81 times as long for cattle dogs and 1.09 for Labradors). The direction still matches Markowitz from a different country and dataset: describe the behavior, not the trait.

**Struggles as behaviors.** Nakamura's negative trait words carried the largest penalties among the labels: "dominant" 1.46, "sensitive" 1.42, "hyperactive" 1.32, "timid" 1.20, "submissive" 1.16. The study cannot separate the word from the dog it describes, so read this as "the trait label is the costliest way to report a struggle," not "the word causes the stay." Listings that named a trait to deny it ("not dominant," 24 listings; "not hyperactive," 34) also ran long. `SKILL.md` writes each struggle as a behavior a reader can picture and never names a trait to deny it.

**Placement constraints.** "Only dog" ran 1.57 times as long and "no cats" 1.37. The constraint is a fact about the dog and is published as a search filter either way, so `SKILL.md` keeps it; the finding marks constrained dogs as the ones that need the most promotion, not the ones whose sentence should go.

**Positive, creative wording within these limits.** Markowitz's findings rule out emotional appeals, superlatives, and social words. They do not rule out a lively, positive account of observed behavior: no study here tests humor, a nickname, or vivid verbs on their own. `SKILL.md` allows them when each rests on something the dog does (an editorial decision).

**Brevity.** In Study 1, word count was positively associated with days online (β = .03): longer profiles stayed up longer. The effect is small. `SKILL.md` still holds profiles to 175-250 words and penalizes going more than 20% past that; the steepness is an editorial decision rather than a measured effect, and the rule against trading a disclosure for length stands.

**Photos, with mixed evidence.** In Study 2, adopted pets' profiles carried more photos than unadopted pets' profiles. In Study 1, the number of photos was positively associated with days online (β = .04). The study does not settle which way photos push, so the photo target in `SKILL.md` rests on Study 2 and on Markowitz's argument that photos reduce an online reader's uncertainty, not on a measured effect.

## What deters interest

**Social and humanizing words.** In Study 1, profiles with higher rates of social words stayed online longer (β = .05), and in Study 2, low rates of social words best predicted that a profile belonged to an adopted pet. Markowitz's examples of social words include "boy," "girl," "friend," "soulmate," and "sweetheart." The papers measure the association and do not test why it occurs.

**Conditions on the adopter.** *Adopters Welcome* says requirements such as fenced yards "significantly decrease the pool of adopters." Neither study tests adopter conditions in a listing.

The skill applies this to conditions on the *adopter*, which is where the mechanism operates: an elimination question shrinks the applicant pool while telling nobody anything about the animal. It does not apply to constraints that are true of the animal, and it does not override the one-struggle floor. See "Placement constraints stay" in `SKILL.md`.

**Euphemism for why the animal is at the shelter.** Kelling tested "her last owners surrendered her" against "her last owners could not meet all her needs," and the two wordings changed how readers explained the surrender. 45 participants volunteered that they wanted to know why the dog was at the shelter.

**Promises in place of observations.** *Adopters Welcome* asks counselors to be "transparent with facts and observations, while avoiding promises." McGuire et al. (2020), *Animals* 10(9), 1702, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7552731/, found that dogs a shelter evaluation flagged for resource guarding guarded in their adoptive homes 47.4% of the time, so a behavior seen at the shelter is worth reporting and a forecast built on it often fails.

## The takeaway

Profiles of adopted pets use more analytic language and fewer social words than profiles of unadopted pets, and readers in an experiment preferred the analytic version. The effects are small and correlational at scale, and the experiment measured stated intent rather than adoptions. That is enough to rule out emotional appeals and social words, and it is why `SKILL.md` says to follow the rules when instinct prefers emotional copy. It does not rule out telling observed behavior as a warm, lively scene.

## Where the two-struggle cap comes from

`SKILL.md` has every card name one or two light struggles and sends the rest to the pre-meet history review. This is an editorial decision, and no study here measures it in either direction:

- **Markowitz** measured language style (analytic against storytelling, social words), not how many difficulties a profile disclosed.
- **Kelling** measured what readers understood from mock ads and what they said they wanted. Readers ranked behavior first among the information on offer (mean rank 1.97, first for 50.5% of 546 respondents), but the study did not follow any ad to an adoption, so it cannot say whether a card with five struggles places better or worse than one with two.
- **Humane World and Best Friends** both steer the profile toward promotion and put the full behavior history in the adoption conversation.

The cap rests on what one shelter's cards showed. Cards for harder dogs that listed every difficulty in the record read as mostly problems: one draft named five struggles in 64 of 238 words. A card that named two light ones, said where the jumping happens and that it is improving, and read as a dog worth meeting. The one-struggle floor keeps the card honest; the history review carries what the card leaves out.

## Where the serious-incident rule comes from

`SKILL.md` routes five kinds of serious incident to the pre-meet consultation: a bite, an attack on another animal, guarding that made contact, anything involving a child, and a serious injury to a cat or small animal. Each needs its circumstances explained and questions answered, which a person in a consultation can do and a web page cannot.

No study tests whether a bite history belongs in public copy, in either direction, so treat any claim that publishing one changes clicks, applications, or returns as unsupported. The rule rests on peer practice, with one wording point from Kelling:

- **Keeping the incident off the card is the industry norm.** No national body instructs one in the listing. Humane World for Animals and Best Friends both put disclosure in the adoption conversation and steer the profile toward promotion. Some rescues publish bites in the profile body anyway, so a reader who has seen their cards may expect it.
- **A struggle on the card has to stay recognizable.** Readers of Kelling's "cute" ads identified the stated behavioral challenge less often than readers of the plain ads: dog aggression 87.5% of the time from "tends to display aggression towards other dogs" and 52.2% from "is picky about his friends." That is a comprehension finding, not an adoption one, and it is why the one or two struggles that stay on the card carry a concrete example under their gentle framing.
- **Profiles that publish incidents are the counterexample, not the model.** They tend to show the failures the language rules target: clinical vocabulary published raw, adopter screening stacked onto a disclosure, handling prescribed to the adopter, and one incident retold in place of the pattern.

## Where the softened-constraint rule comes from

Best Friends Animal Society's "Animal Shelter Dogs: Promoting Them for Adoption" is the industry's model for adoption bios, and the rules here split from it on one technique while agreeing with it on three others.

**The split.** On constraints it instructs: "You can include restrictions, but be gentle and choose your words carefully. For example, instead of saying 'Andy must go to a home without children,' which could scare off even a childless couple, put a positive spin on it: 'Jo-Jo's such a goofy bundle of energy that he might be a bit too much for small children.'" Strip the charm from that rewrite and no constraint is left standing. The reasoning behind the instruction is about the applicant pool rather than the reader's understanding, and it does not hold here, because shelter-management systems publish the constraint as a structured attribute either way. The softening costs the reader the fact and saves the funnel nothing.

The guide works the same move on behavior. It rejects "Joey unfortunately has leash reactivity, which means he behaves offensively toward other dogs when he's on walks. He can live peaceably with other dogs, as long as they're submissive" in favor of "Joey is that rare sort of pooch whom a child can climb all over and hug. He's so gentle and sweet around everyone. Well, except other dogs when he's out on a walk. He's not so good with other pooches when he's on leash." Two of its three objections to the first version hold here: "leash reactivity" is shelter vocabulary, and "as long as they're submissive" is a condition on the adopter. The third does not. The rewrite moves the behavior behind two clauses of praise and lands on "not so good with other pooches," which names no behavior a reader can picture. Fix the jargon and the adopter condition, keep the plain clause, and add the example: "Barks and pulls toward other dogs he passes on walks."

**The agreement.** Three of its instructions match the rules above and are worth knowing are not idiosyncratic to one shelter: keep the language "simple and conversational; avoid animal welfare jargon or technical terms whenever possible"; open on something specific rather than the list of attributes it gives as the failure case ("Joey is a five-year-old neutered male pittie/Lab mix with white markings, up-to-date on shots"); and close by inviting contact rather than screening for it.
