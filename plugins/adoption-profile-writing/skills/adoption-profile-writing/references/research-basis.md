# Why These Rules

The evidence behind the language rules in `SKILL.md`. Read it when a rule looks wrong, or when someone asks why profiles are written this way.

Two primary studies, plus two guides for the rules the studies do not cover:

- **Markowitz (2020)**, *Journal of Applied Social Psychology* 50(3), 160–173, https://doi.org/10.1111/jasp.12647. Study 1 tracked how long 184,805 Petfinder profiles stayed online. Study 2 compared 676,004 profiles of adopted and unadopted pets. Study 3 was an experiment with 987 readers. The author describes the effect sizes as small.
- **Kelling et al. (2026)**, *Society & Animals* 34(2), 179–199, https://doi.org/10.1163/15685306-bja10192, published online 2024. 561 readers rated mock dog adoption ads, with "plain" and "cute" wordings of the same behavioral challenge.
- **Humane World for Animals, *Adopters Welcome* (2025)**, https://humanepro.org/sites/default/files/documents/guides-adopters-welcome-2025-final-digital.pdf. The source for the rule against conditions on the adopter.
- **Best Friends Animal Society, "Animal Shelter Dogs: Promoting Them for Adoption"**, https://bestfriends.org/network/resources-tools/animal-shelter-dogs-promoting-them-adoption. The source for the opening and closing rules. See the last section below.

## What increases adoption interest

**Analytic, fact-based writing.** In Study 2, adopted pets' profiles used more analytic language, measured by articles ("a," "the") and prepositions, than unadopted pets' profiles. Markowitz counts pronouns, adverbs, conjunctions, and negations as storytelling words, the opposite pole. In Study 3, readers of an analytic, low-social profile said they were more likely to adopt the pet (Cohen's d = .18) and to visit the shelter (d = .16) than readers of a storytelling, high-social profile.

**Behavior information.** Kelling's readers ranked behavior as the most important information in an ad: mean rank 1.97, ranked first by 50.5% of 546 respondents, ahead of breed, age, the reason the dog was at the shelter, sex, and weight. Readers who wanted more from an ad most often wanted more about the dog's behavior.

**Plain wording of a behavior.** Kelling's softened "cute" wordings hid the behavior they described. Readers identified dog aggression 87.5% of the time from "tends to display aggression towards other dogs" and 52.2% of the time from "is picky about his friends"; housetraining 83.9% of the time from "had a few accidents in the house" and 57.7% from "does best on a schedule." "High energy" was identified by about half of readers in either version (52.1% plain, 53.2% cute), and some readers did not know what "zoomies" meant.

**Brevity.** In Study 1, word count was positively associated with days online (β = .03): longer profiles stayed up longer. The effect is small, which is why `SKILL.md` treats length as a weak signal and refuses to trade a disclosure for it.

**Photos, with mixed evidence.** In Study 2, adopted pets' profiles carried more photos than unadopted pets' profiles. In Study 1, the number of photos was positively associated with days online (β = .04). The study does not settle which way photos push, so the photo target in `SKILL.md` rests on Study 2 and on Markowitz's argument that photos reduce an online reader's uncertainty, not on a measured effect.

## What deters interest

**Social and humanizing words.** In Study 1, profiles with higher rates of social words stayed online longer (β = .05), and in Study 2, low rates of social words best predicted that a profile belonged to an adopted pet. Markowitz's examples of social words include "boy," "girl," "friend," "soulmate," and "sweetheart." The papers measure the association and do not test why it occurs.

**Conditions on the adopter.** *Adopters Welcome* says requirements such as fenced yards "significantly decrease the pool of adopters." Neither study tests adopter conditions in a listing.

The skill applies this to conditions on the *adopter*, which is where the mechanism operates: an elimination question shrinks the applicant pool while telling nobody anything about the animal. It does not apply to constraints that are true of the animal, and it does not override the disclosure floor. See "Placement constraints stay" in `SKILL.md`.

**Euphemism for why the animal is at the shelter.** Kelling tested "her last owners surrendered her" against "her last owners could not meet all her needs," and the two wordings changed how readers explained the surrender. 45 participants volunteered that they wanted to know why the dog was at the shelter.

**Promises in place of observations.** *Adopters Welcome* asks counselors to be "transparent with facts and observations, while avoiding promises." McGuire et al. (2020), *Animals* 10(9), 1702, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7552731/, found that dogs a shelter evaluation flagged for resource guarding guarded in their adoptive homes 47.4% of the time, so a behavior seen at the shelter is worth reporting and a forecast built on it often fails.

## The takeaway

Profiles of adopted pets use more analytic language and fewer social words than profiles of unadopted pets, and readers in an experiment preferred the analytic version. The effects are small and correlational at scale, and the experiment measured stated intent rather than adoptions. That is enough to set the default register, and it is why `SKILL.md` says to follow the rules when instinct prefers warm copy.

## Where the serious-incident rule comes from

`SKILL.md` routes five kinds of serious incident to the pre-meet consultation: a bite, an attack on another animal, guarding that made contact, anything involving a child, and a serious injury to a cat or small animal. Each needs its circumstances explained and questions answered, which a person in a consultation can do and a web page cannot.

No study tests whether a bite history belongs in public copy, in either direction, so treat any claim that publishing one changes clicks, applications, or returns as unsupported. The rule rests on peer practice and on Kelling's findings:

- **Keeping the incident off the card is the industry norm.** No national body instructs one in the listing. Humane World for Animals and Best Friends both put disclosure in the adoption conversation and steer the profile toward promotion. Some rescues publish bites in the profile body anyway, so a reader who has seen their cards may expect it.
- **Kelling supports the plain-words rule directly.** Readers of the study's "cute" ads identified the stated behavioral challenge less well than readers of the plain ads. Softening does not merely sound better; it measurably stops the disclosure from landing. That finding is why the behaviors that do stay on the card carry a concrete example rather than a label.
- **Profiles that publish incidents are the counterexample, not the model.** They tend to show the failures the language rules target: clinical vocabulary published raw, adopter screening stacked onto a disclosure, handling prescribed to the adopter, and one incident retold in place of the pattern.

## Where the softened-constraint rule comes from

Best Friends Animal Society's "Animal Shelter Dogs: Promoting Them for Adoption" is the industry's model for adoption bios, and the rules here split from it on one technique while agreeing with it on three others.

**The split.** On constraints it instructs: "You can include restrictions, but be gentle and choose your words carefully. For example, instead of saying 'Andy must go to a home without children,' which could scare off even a childless couple, put a positive spin on it: 'Jo-Jo's such a goofy bundle of energy that he might be a bit too much for small children.'" Run the delete test on that rewrite and nothing survives: strike the charm and no constraint is left standing. The reasoning behind the instruction is about the applicant pool rather than the reader's understanding, and it does not hold here, because shelter-management systems publish the constraint as a structured attribute either way. The softening costs the reader the fact and saves the funnel nothing.

The guide works the same move on behavior. It rejects "Joey unfortunately has leash reactivity, which means he behaves offensively toward other dogs when he's on walks. He can live peaceably with other dogs, as long as they're submissive" in favor of "Joey is that rare sort of pooch whom a child can climb all over and hug. He's so gentle and sweet around everyone. Well, except other dogs when he's out on a walk. He's not so good with other pooches when he's on leash." Two of its three objections to the first version hold here: "leash reactivity" is shelter vocabulary, and "as long as they're submissive" is a condition on the adopter. The third does not. The rewrite moves the behavior behind two clauses of praise and lands on "not so good with other pooches," which names no behavior a reader can picture. Fix the jargon and the adopter condition, keep the plain clause, and add the example: "Barks and pulls toward other dogs he passes on walks."

**The agreement.** Three of its instructions match the rules above and are worth knowing are not idiosyncratic to one shelter: keep the language "simple and conversational; avoid animal welfare jargon or technical terms whenever possible"; open on something specific rather than the list of attributes it gives as the failure case ("Joey is a five-year-old neutered male pittie/Lab mix with white markings, up-to-date on shots"); and close by inviting contact rather than screening for it.
