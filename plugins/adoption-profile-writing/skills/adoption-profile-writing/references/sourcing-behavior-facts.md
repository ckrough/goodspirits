# Where the Behavior Facts Come From

Every claim in a profile traces to the animal's record. This file names which system holds which notes, how to weigh a record that spans years, and when to stop and ask.

## The sources, richest first

**The shelter-management system's memos or notes page** (ShelterLuv, PetPoint, and similar). Usually the fullest record a shelter keeps. Look for bite history, cat-test notes, matchmaking or ideal-home notes, and behavior or trainer notes.

**Volunteer walk notes**, wherever the shelter keeps them, filtered by the animal's ID. Ask the person you are writing for to paste in the notes.

**The foster case file**, when the animal has been fostered. These are source documents: strictly factual, no embellishment, and they hold the full record including everything a public profile omits.

**The current live profile**, when rewriting rather than writing fresh. Ask for the existing copy if the request did not include it.

## Read the record in time order

A shelter record is not a flat set of facts. It is a history, and for a long-stay animal it is often a history of change. Sort the entries by date before drawing any conclusion from them.

**Weight recent observations over early ones.** A dog's first weeks after intake show a dog in transition, not the dog an adopter will live with. Where the record logs a behavior repeatedly across months and the entries show a direction, the recent end of that line is the animal. A dog recorded as highly reactive in his first fortnight, calmer at six weeks, and described by several volunteers a year later as getting along well with other dogs is a dog who acclimated. Write the dog he is now. The intake-week behavior is not a disclosure the adopter needs, because it describes a situation that no longer exists.

**Recent notes supersede an old claim only when they cover the same situation.** Age alone does not retire an observation, because silence is not a contradiction. The question to ask about an entry two or more years old is not how old it is but whether anything recent looked at the same thing. The recent record gives one of three answers:

| What the recent record does | The claim is | What to do with it |
|---|---|---|
| Observes that situation, and the behavior no longer appears in it | Superseded | Drop it. It describes a dog who no longer exists. |
| Does not observe that situation at all | Unrefreshed | Do not publish it as current, and do not quietly drop it either. Raise it with the person you are writing for. |
| Echoes the behavior | Standing | Publish it. |

The distinction the table turns on is "recent notes" against "recent notes *of the same situation*." A dog with forty walk notes from the last six months and no feeding note in two years has an unrefreshed food-guarding claim, not a superseded one: nobody watched him eat, and the walk notes say nothing about the bowl however many of them there are.

**Thin recent notes are not evidence that nothing changed.** That is the reasoning the middle row exists to stop. Volunteers writing up a long-stay animal tend to stop logging: the dog is familiar, there is nothing new to say, and the log goes quiet. So a record whose last three entries are eighteen months old is a record with a gap in it, not a record of a dog who stopped developing. The gap is a fact about who was writing rather than about the animal, and reading it as improvement is how a real disclosure leaves a card. Say so rather than treating the silence as data.

**Serious incidents do not decay.** The five listed under "Serious incidents go to the consultation" in `SKILL.md` stand however old the entry is and whatever the recent record does or does not say, and recency weighting never buries one. None goes on the card, but each one still decides the closing line and any placement limit it supports, so find it regardless of its date.

## When to stop and ask

Four situations are for shelter staff to settle, not the writer. In each, tell the person you are writing for which entries or tags conflict and how, so they can take it to staff, and wait:

1. **A tag in the shelter-management system contradicts the written observations.** The tag may be stale, and a wrong one removes the animal from every search that filters on it. Staff set the tags and staff change them.
2. **The record contradicts itself on a placement constraint.** A single-pet requirement and an active bonded-pair disclaimer in the same file cannot both be written into one card.
3. **A behavior appears to have evolved but the evidence is thin.** Staff often know an animal's current behavior before it reaches the file, so the answer may exist and simply not be written down.
4. **An old behavior has no recent observation either way.** The unrefreshed row above. Name the behavior, the date of the last entry recording it, and what the recent notes cover instead, so staff can answer from what they have seen rather than from the file.

Write everything the record does settle, and raise the open item alongside the draft. Do not resolve any of the four by picking the reading that makes the copy easier.

## When sources disagree on history

The memos page, the behavior profile, and the behavior team's summary contradict each other on dates and history often enough that finding a conflict is not a signal that something is wrong. Prefer what the shelter observed directly over what a previous owner reported. People who can answer a follow-up question made the shelter's observations. Nobody can check a previous owner's report, and the patterns-not-moments rule forbids attributing it in public copy anyway.

## Names and dates

The record names volunteers, staff, fosters, and adopters, and it carries incident dates. None of that reaches public copy: the patterns-not-moments rule in `SKILL.md` cuts the date and the occasion, and internal-record specificity (staff or volunteer names, other adoptable animals by name, case-file phrasing) stays internal.

Staff and volunteer contact details, wherever they appear in the record, are sensitive. Never reproduce a personal phone number or email address in a draft or in anything that leaves the shelter.
