# Writing an Adoption Profile

This is the source of truth for writing adopter-facing copy. Prefer it over inventing structure or reasoning from instinct: several of its rules are counterintuitive, and the research behind them is in `references/research-basis.md`.

`[Name]` stands for the animal's name and `[Shelter]` for the shelter or rescue publishing the profile. Replace both in every draft.

## What this governs

This describes the process for writing copy that an adopter reads about a specific animal: the profile body on the shelter's website, kennel cards, published adoption listings, and social posts.

It does not govern the animal's record. Observation logs and behavior notes are source documents, strictly factual and with no embellishment, and they keep detail this skill translates or drops. Marketing interpretation happens downstream of them, here.

## Procedure

1. **Gather the record**, and read it in time order. Behavior facts come from the animal's own file, never from inference. If the request does not include the record, ask the person you are writing for to paste it in: the shelter-management system's memos, walk notes, any foster notes, and the current profile when this is a rewrite. Do not draft from a name, a photo, or a breed alone. `references/sourcing-behavior-facts.md` names where each kind of note usually lives, how to weigh an evolving record, and when to stop and ask.
2. **Check the record for the five serious incidents** under "Serious incidents go to the consultation" below. None of them goes on the card, but each one decides the closing line, and a child-related incident usually carries a placement limit that does go on the card. Catching them before drafting is cheaper than auditing for them after.
3. **Choose the one or two struggles the card will name**, under "Name one or two light struggles" below. Everything else the record holds goes to the history review, which makes the closing line required.
4. **Draft the four sections defined in `references/profile-template.md`**, in the order that file gives: ABOUT THIS PET, HOUSEHOLD FIT, TRAINING, GOOD TO KNOW. The template owns the structure, what each section covers, the closing lines, and the seven topics a finished card has to answer. Each section there carries "What to write" and "Before moving on" checks, and the file includes a finished example. The example is illustrative: take its section structure and its warm, specific register, and write this animal's own openings, sentences, and length from its record.
5. **Run the disclosure pass.** It runs after drafting rather than during it, because both halves of it, what must appear and what must not, are easier to enforce against sentences that exist than to hold in mind while composing them.
6. **Apply the language rules.**
7. **Run the checks under "Before you return"** at the end of this file.

## The disclosure rules

Three groups. **What must appear** decides whether a fact ships. **How it is written** decides the wording of every fact that does. **When to stop and ask** covers the one case that is not the writer's to resolve.

### What must appear

#### Name one or two light struggles

Every card names at least one struggle and no more than two. One keeps the card honest: a profile with nothing hard in it reads as a profile that is hiding something, and the reader stops trusting the good parts. More than two turns the card into a list of problems: one draft for a harder dog named five struggles, which took 64 of its 238 words (27%), and a reader came away with a picture of the problems rather than the dog. The model is a published card that names two struggles a reader can picture, where they happen, and that one is improving, in 35 of 233 words (15%).

Choose the two from the record:

1. **Prefer what an adopter would notice in daily life** (jumping when excited, pulling on leash, barking at the door) over what shows up only in a specific setting or under handling.
2. **Prefer struggles that come with context or progress in the record** ("mostly in his kennel," "getting better with training"), so the sentence can end on something true and hopeful.
3. **A behavior mentioned anywhere on the card counts toward the two.** Leash barking at dogs in HOUSEHOLD FIT is one of them. Placement constraints ("would do best in a home without young children"), unknowns ("has not been tested with cats"), and the support line do not count.

Everything else goes to the history review. Staff go over it with the adopter before the meet, where a person can give the circumstances and answer questions. Holding a struggle back makes the closing line required (see "The closing line is required" below), so the card still tells the reader that a conversation is waiting.

This is an editorial decision, not a research finding. No study measures how many struggles a card should carry.

#### One occurrence is not a pattern

A behavior recorded once is not yet a description of the animal, and publishing it describes a dog who does not exist. A dog frightened during one thunderstorm has told you about one thunderstorm. Look for recurrence before you write, and when a claim rests on a single entry that nothing else in the record echoes, leave it out.

This rule cuts anecdotes from a card. It is not an instruction to audit the card for a missing line.

#### Serious incidents go to the consultation, not onto the card

Five kinds of incident stay off the card however the record words them, and the shelter discusses them with the adopter in a consultation before they meet the animal:

1. A bite, to a person or another animal. A snap that connected is a bite.
2. An attack on another animal.
3. Guarding that made contact with a person or another animal. Contact is the line: guarding that stopped short of it (a growl, a stiffen, a snap that did not connect) is an ordinary struggle, and it competes for the card's two places like any other.
4. Anything involving a child.
5. A kill or serious injury to a cat or a small animal.

In the consultation a person can state the circumstances and the date and answer what the adopter asks back, which a web page cannot do. One entry is enough to route an incident there, and the entry does not decay with age: `references/sourcing-behavior-facts.md` covers why recency weighting never buries one.

**The incident goes; the limit it supports stays.** A child-related incident usually carries a placement limit, and that limit is true of the animal, so it publishes in HOUSEHOLD FIT as a recommendation about the home: "He is more comfortable with older teens and adults, and would do best in a home without young children." The incident behind it, and whoever reported it, does not. The same holds for a "no cats" or "only dog" limit behind an attack.

**The closing line is required when the record holds one, or when the card held back a struggle.** With nothing about the incident or the held-back struggles on the card, the closer is the only signal an adopter gets that a conversation is waiting for them:

- Most animals: end GOOD TO KNOW with "[Shelter] will review [Name]'s full history with you before you meet [him/her]."
- A Diamond Dog: end with the Diamond Dog message in `references/profile-template.md`, word for word. It carries the history-review sentence along with the program support and the way to meet the dog, so the card has no separate history line.

| Keep | Cut |
|---|---|
| "[Shelter] will review his full history with you before you meet him." | "[Shelter] will review his full history with you before you take him home." |

The cut phrasing assumes the adoption is happening and puts the disclosure at the last step. The consultation comes before the introduction, so "before you meet him" describes when the conversation actually happens.

The card's silence works only if the consultation really comes before the adopter meets the animal. If the record suggests it did not, tell the person you are writing for so they can raise it with shelter staff.

#### Placement constraints stay, and cost nothing

Constraints that are true of the animal ("only dog," "no cats," "a home without young children") stay in the copy. Shelter-management systems usually publish them as structured attributes and push them to listing sites such as Petfinder and Adopt-a-Pet, where they drive the search filters, so deleting the sentence widens the funnel by nobody. It only hides the reason from the adopter and makes the prose disagree with the attribute panel printed beside it.

The question to ask about a constraint is provenance, not presence: does the record support it? See "When the filter and the record disagree" below when it does not.

**Do not spin a constraint into a compliment.** The standard rewrite in shelter promotion guidance turns "must go to a home without children" into a charm sentence, on the theory that the plain version scares off even childless readers. Strip the charm from it and no constraint is left: a reader can no longer say what the limit is.

| Keep | Cut |
|---|---|
| "He is more comfortable with older teens and adults, and would do best in a home without young children." | "He's such a goofy bundle of energy that he might be a bit too much for small children." |

The same guidance says to lead with positives and put the challenge last. Ordering is fine; burying is not. A behavior that arrives at the tail of a sentence about how gentle the animal is has been staged so the reader discounts it.

Conditions on the *applicant* are a different thing and do not belong in a profile: "qualified adopters only," "must have a fenced yard," "experienced owners only," "serious inquiries only." Removing one of those costs no adopter a good match, because it says nothing about the animal. That is the mechanism: an elimination question shrinks the applicant pool while telling nobody anything about the dog.

### How it is written

#### Plain words, not clinical ones

Write the behavior the way a person without training would say it, at about an eighth-grade reading level (Humane World for Animals, 2025). "Resource guarding," "reactivity," "redirection," "barrier frustration," and "arousal" are the shelter's working vocabulary and mean little to the public. Some of them sound more alarming than the behavior is; others sound like nothing at all.

| The record's words | What gets published |
|---|---|
| Jumpy and mouthy in kennel | Can get jumpy when he is excited. The jumping mostly happens in his kennel at the shelter, and it is getting better with training. |
| Leash reactive to dogs | Barks and pulls toward other dogs he passes on walks. |
| Stranger-reactive at the kennel door | Takes a little time to warm up to new people: he barks when someone new comes to his door, and settles once they give him time and space. |
| Resource guards food and high-value treats | Likes to eat in peace, and may growl if someone reaches toward his bowl. |

**Phrase each struggle as positively as the facts allow.** Show it as a scene, and add what the record says about where it happens, what has been seen to help, or how it is changing. A gentle opening clause ("takes a little time to warm up") tells the reader how to hold the information; the example after it tells them what it looks like. Each positive clause is itself an observation with a source. It is never a prescription for the adopter ("needs a home that...") or a promise ("he will grow out of it").

**Write a struggle as a behavior, never as a trait.** "Timid," "sensitive," "dominant," "hyperactive," "reactive," "anxious," and "stubborn" name a kind of dog; the behavior names a moment a reader can picture and judge for their own home. Across 70,733 listings, negative trait words went with the longest stays of any wording tested: "dominant" 1.46 times as long, "sensitive" 1.42, "hyperactive" 1.32, "timid" 1.20 (Nakamura et al., 2019). "Takes a little time to warm up: he barks when someone new comes to his door, and settles once they give him space" carries the same fact as "timid" without the label.

**The behavior stays recognizable.** After the gentle framing, a reader should still be able to say what the dog does. "Takes a little time to warm up: he barks when someone new comes to his door" passes. "Is picky about his friends" names no behavior a reader can picture, and fails.

#### Patterns, not moments

Every behavior statement describes how the animal generally is. None retells one occasion.

An observation log records moments; a profile publishes the pattern behind them. "She will chase a flying disc for forty minutes" is the pattern; "one volunteer had to end a disc session after fifty minutes" is the moment that demonstrated it, and the moment does not ship. This binds favorable copy exactly as it binds disclosures.

**Publish no incident specifics.** No dates, no handler or volunteer, no what-preceded-it, no body-language detail such as a lip curl or a startle.

**Never attribute a behavior to a previous owner or family.** Their reports are private information, and a line like "his previous family reported incidents with young children" publishes both an incident and its source. The pattern survives the translation; the source and the occasion do not: "He is more comfortable with older teens and adults, and would do best in a home without young children."

The full record stays in the foster file and the behavior logs.

**Say why the dog is at the shelter when it was no fault of the dog's.** Many shelter dogs arrive through circumstances that had nothing to do with them: an owner who moved, a landlord who would not allow dogs, an owner's illness or death, a stray intake, a transfer from a crowded shelter. Readers ask for this (Kelling et al., 2026), and a reader who is not told fills the gap with a guess that usually blames the dog. Where the record carries a reason like these, state it plainly in one clause in ABOUT THIS PET: "[Name] came to [Shelter] when his family moved somewhere that did not allow dogs." Where the reason is an incident, a conflict, or anything that would identify or characterize the surrendering family, the rule above governs and it does not ship; the card then says nothing about it.

What is never written is the softened middle. "Her last owners could not meet all her needs" reads as a fault in the dog where "her last owners surrendered her" reads as a circumstance. Say the plain thing or say nothing.

#### Disclose the observation, not the prediction

A behavior seen at the shelter or in foster is a fact about that setting. Writing it as a property the animal carries into the reader's home is a prediction, and the prediction is usually wrong. In the study behind this rule, a shelter assessment of resource guarding was right 47.4% of the time: more than half of the dogs flagged as guarders by staff or by their surrendering owner did not guard in their adoptive homes. The behavior is worth publishing, and the forecast built on it is not.

| Keep | Cut |
|---|---|
| "He guards his bowl at the kennel and may growl if someone reaches toward it" | "He will guard food from you" |
| "He has gone after another dog through the kennel fence" | "He will always need an experienced handler" |
| "He barks at visitors and takes time to warm up" | "He will never be comfortable with strangers" |

This is the mirror of the promise rule in the language section. "Great with all dogs" fails because it promises a good outcome the record cannot support; "will always need management" fails the same way, in the same sentence position, about a bad one.

#### Disclose behavior, never prescribe handling

A profile states what the animal does. It does not tell the adopter how to handle it. The adoption team delivers training guidance, management plans, and handling protocols during the consult, where it can tailor them to the actual home. A web page cannot, and a management plan read cold reads as a list of demands on the adopter.

| Keep | Cut |
|---|---|
| "He guards his bowl at the shelter and may growl if someone reaches toward it" | "so he needs a feeding station he can be called away from" |
| "On leash he barks and pulls toward other dogs" | "which is easier to manage when he wears a harness" |
| "She jumps up on people when she is excited" | "so turn away until all four paws are on the floor" |
| "He barks at visitors and takes time to warm up" | "so he needs a space of his own when guests are over" |

After drafting, reread every "so...", "he needs...", and "he does best with..." clause. Each one is prescription unless it states a placement constraint.

Name the program the animal is enrolled in (such as the Diamond Dog Program), and list the cues it knows. Both describe the animal rather than instruct the adopter, so both stay.

**The failure to watch for is the compound one: a disclosure with an elimination clause attached to it.** Two rules already forbid each half, "Placement constraints stay" for the screen and this section for the instruction, but they arrive together and in one breath because a writer who has just published something alarming reaches for a sentence that makes it safe. A typical example: "She has bitten when her boundaries weren't respected. For this reason, she is looking for a confident, experienced pet parent who uses positive reinforcement." The bite belongs in the consultation rather than on the card, and everything after "for this reason" is an elimination question, on a senior dog whose reader pool needs widening rather than narrowing. Publish the behavior that belongs on the card, and stop. The reader decides whether they are the right home; the card does not decide it for them.

### When to stop and ask

#### When the filter and the record disagree, ask

The tags in the shelter-management system are set by staff who have met the animal, and they are the operating default: write copy consistent with the tag. But a tag that contradicts the written observations is worth catching, because it may be stale, and because a wrong tag silently removes the animal from every search that filters on it.

When a tag and the record disagree, or when the record contradicts itself on a constraint, **stop and ask rather than resolving it in the draft.** Say which tag, which entries, and how they conflict. The person you are writing for takes it to shelter staff. Never flip a tag's meaning in the prose on your own reading of the file, and never quietly drop a constraint because the notes look thin.

A profile does not ship while its prose and its attribute panel disagree. The conflict clears one of two ways, and both need staff: either the tag is wrong and staff update it, leaving the prose to stand, or the tag is right and the observation behind the prose was partial, so the prose changes to agree with the tag.

## Language rules

The research finding these rest on is counterintuitive: emotional, anthropomorphizing copy underperforms factual copy (Markowitz, 2020). `references/research-basis.md` carries the study. That finding is about emotional appeals and social words, not about warmth: a card can be positive, lively, and creative as long as each sentence rests on something the dog does. When instinct and these rules conflict, follow the rules.

- **Factual, not emotional.** Every sentence carries something a reader could check. "Look at the cuteness! This boy is adorable and full of love" is the register to avoid.
- **Positive and creative, built on behavior.** Lead with what the dog does well, and give it energy: vivid verbs, a specific setting, a nickname or a light line that rests on an observed behavior ("the king of fetch," "will roll over for belly rubs the first chance he gets"). The test is the source, not the tone: a playful line about a real behavior passes, and a warm line with no behavior behind it ("full of love") does not.
- **Show what being with the animal is like.** Tell the observations from the side of someone spending time with the dog: shared moments with people or other dogs, contact, play, routines, and what happened next. "She stretches out on the couch beside whoever is reading and rests her chin on their knee. She leans into a hand that scratches her ears and rolls onto her back for belly rubs as soon as someone sits on the floor" lets a reader picture an afternoon with her. "She knows sit, down, wait, and touch, and she is learning stay. She sits the moment she is asked and works eagerly for treats." is accurate and reads as an evaluator's log of how she performs. Both are observations; the first is the one to aim for. GOOD TO KNOW follows the same register: name each struggle so a reader can picture it, then end on where it happens or how it is improving. A scene uses only what the record holds: never add a detail to make a sentence flow, never join two separate observations into one scene, and never write an inner state ("he dreams of," "he knows he is home") that nobody observed.
- **Cut platitudes and filler.** "Forever home," "so much love to give," "heart of gold," "won't last long." Test each phrase by deleting it: if no fact or setting goes with it, it was filler. `scripts/check_draft.py` lists the common ones.
- **Concrete behavior over personality labels, positive ones included.** Describe what the animal does, not what it is like. In 70,733 listings, the kind words went with longer stays too: "friendly" 1.14 times as long, "gentle" 1.14, "quiet" 1.15, "obedient" 1.24, "trainable" 1.25 (Nakamura et al., 2019). "Protective," "loyal," and "affectionate" are labels a reader cannot check, and so are inner states: "calm," "at ease," "content," "happy." Write the behavior that showed the trait ("leans against you and rolls over for belly rubs," "lies down in the sun once outside") instead.
- **Show energy as activity.** "Energetic" and "active" went with longer stays (1.22 and 1.16 times as long); "lively" and "eager" went with shorter ones (0.88 and 0.92). Write what the dog does, for how long, and how it looks doing it: "fetches for over an hour without slowing down," "wrestles and chases in playgroup." An upbeat word belongs beside that behavior ("lively in playgroup, where he wrestles and chases"), not in place of it.
- **Never name a trait to deny it.** "Not aggressive," "no resource guarding," and "doesn't have a mean bone" plant the question they answer, and listings that said "not dominant" or "not hyperactive" ran long. Say what the dog does instead, or say nothing.
- **Record gaps go to staff, not into the copy.** "The ages are not recorded" and "his listing marks this as to be determined" beside a tag read as contradicting the tag. State what the tag and the record say, and put the gap in your open items.
- **Plain vocabulary over shelter vocabulary.** See the table above.
- **Open on identity, not statistics.** The first sentence says who the animal is. "You haven't lived until you've had Sammy's nose on your lap" beats "Sammy is a 5-year-old shepherd mix with hip dysplasia."
- **Drop social and humanizing words.** "Buddy," "friend," "helper," "sweetheart," "best friend," "companion," and the pet-as-person nicknames: "lady," "gentleman," "angel," "princess," "old soul," "shadow," "velcro dog," "love bug," "fur baby." These correlate with animals that did not get adopted.
- **Drop superlatives and absolute claims.** "Extremely," "adorable," "the perfect," "100%," "loves everyone," "always friendly," "never aggressive." The absolutes promise an outcome the record cannot back.
- **Drop scolding and gatekeeping closers.** "Qualified adopters only," "serious inquiries only," "no apartments," "must apply." For a Diamond Dog the Diamond Dog message is the closer; otherwise the history-review line is, when the record needs one. Encourage a response rather than screening for one.
- **No em-dashes.** Use colons, parentheses, or commas.

## When rules pull against each other

The register rules come first: a factual voice, observable behavior instead of personality labels, and observations instead of promises carry most of a profile's quality. Telling those observations as connected scenes and keeping the card inside 175-250 words come next, then covering all seven topics, then keeping out social words and conditions on the adopter. Never trade a covered topic for length: cut filler, platitudes, and repetition first. When a card runs long or reads as mostly problems, a third struggle is the first thing to move to the history review.

Three defects stop a profile from shipping whatever else it does well: no struggle named anywhere, prose that contradicts a tag, and an absolute claim.

## Length and photos

Target 175 to 250 words across the four sections: every word under the four headings, including the support sentence and the closing lines of GOOD TO KNOW. Image and link markup does not count, and neither do program boilerplate blocks (adoption fee, the Senior Care Plan block), which describe the shelter's offer rather than the animal. The Diamond Dog message is about 55 of those words, which leaves about 195 for the rest.

Never buy a shorter card by dropping the card's one or two struggles, and do not pad to reach the floor. A profile that lands under 175 is usually one that skipped a topic rather than one that wrote tightly, so check coverage before adding words. The grader is lenient within 20% of the band (140 to 300 words) and steep past it: a 320-word card loses most of its length points and a 380-word card loses all of them. Most long cards get there through filler and repeated facts, which is where to cut.

Target three to five photos on the profile itself. One or two is too few to reduce an adopter's uncertainty, and that is where most profiles sit. Adding a second and third photo is usually the cheapest improvement left in a finished profile.

Do not count automatically generated galleries, such as a feed of volunteer walk photos appended to every profile. They fill up on their own and usually do not syndicate to listing sites, so a full gallery is not a substitute for putting photos on the profile.

## Before you return

Run these against the finished draft. Each one restates a rule above, gathered here because this is where they fire.

1. **Count the struggles on the whole card.** One or two, each one a behavior a reader can picture, and the struggle sentences under 20% of the narrative words (the support sentence and closing lines do not count toward that share). A third struggle moves to the history review.
2. **Reread every "so...", "he needs...", and "he does best with..." clause.** Each is prescription unless it states a placement constraint.
3. **Check the record once more for the five serious incidents.** Confirm none reached the card in any wording, that any placement limit behind one is stated in HOUSEHOLD FIT, and that the closing line (or the Diamond Dog message) is present whenever an incident or a held-back struggle needs it.
4. **Confirm all four headings exist**, GOOD TO KNOW included, and that the content covers all seven topics: About, Likes, Dogs, Cats, Kids, Training, Struggles. "Untested" covers a topic. Then check the record for a no-fault reason the dog is at the shelter, and state it if the card does not.
5. **Read the prose against the attribute panel.** If they disagree, stop and ask. Do not ship the draft.
6. **Read the first sentence alone.** It names the animal and says something it does, not its breed, age, or medical status.
7. **Read each section for a scene, then for its sources.** A section that reads as a run of standalone attributes needs its observations connected. Then trace every detail in a connected sentence to the record or to the person you are writing for: a scene that reads well because a detail was added or two observations were joined has invented something.
8. **Run the checker on the draft.** Save the draft as markdown with the four headings, then run `python3 scripts/check_draft.py <draft.md>` from this skill's folder (standard library only). It prints PASS or the list to fix: missing headings, word count outside 175 to 250 (and whether it is more than 20% outside), social words, screening phrases, absolute claims, platitudes, em-dashes, struggle sentences over 20% of the card, negative trait words and denials (and the positive labels, listed for review), a support sentence sharing a line with other copy, and a missing or reworded Diamond Dog message. It also reports a page address above ABOUT THIS PET when the draft has one. Fix everything it lists and run it again. If Python is not available, do the same checks by hand against the lists in "Language rules" and say that the script did not run.
