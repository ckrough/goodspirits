# Writing an Adoption Profile

This is the source of truth for writing adopter-facing copy. Prefer it over inventing structure or reasoning from instinct: several of its rules are counterintuitive, and the research behind them is in `references/research-basis.md`.

`[Name]` stands for the animal's name and `[Shelter]` for the shelter or rescue publishing the profile. Replace both in every draft.

## What this governs

This describes the process for writing copy that an adopter reads about a specific animal: the profile body on the shelter's website, kennel cards, published adoption listings, and social posts.

It does not govern the animal's record. Observation logs and behavior notes are source documents, strictly factual and with no embellishment, and they keep detail this skill translates or drops. Marketing interpretation happens downstream of them, here.

## Procedure

1. **Gather the record**, and read it in time order. Behavior facts come from the animal's own file, never from inference. If the request does not include the record, ask the person you are writing for to paste it in: the shelter-management system's memos, walk notes, any foster notes, and the current profile when this is a rewrite. Do not draft from a name, a photo, or a breed alone. `references/sourcing-behavior-facts.md` names where each kind of note usually lives, how to weigh an evolving record, and which open items to flag for staff.
2. **Check the record for the five serious incidents** under "Serious incidents go to the consultation" below. None of them goes on the card, but each one decides the closing line, and a child-related incident usually carries a placement limit that does go on the card. Catching them before drafting is cheaper than auditing for them after.
3. **Choose the one or two struggles the card will name**, under "Name one or two light struggles" below. Everything else the record holds goes to the history review, which makes the closing line required.
4. **Draft the four sections defined in `references/profile-template.md`**, in the order that file gives: ABOUT THIS PET, HOUSEHOLD FIT, TRAINING, GOOD TO KNOW. The template owns the structure, what each section covers, the closing lines, and the seven topics a finished card has to answer. Each section there carries "What to write" and "Before moving on" checks, and the file includes a finished example. The example is illustrative: take its section structure and its warm, specific register, and write this animal's own openings, sentences, and length from its record.
5. **Run the disclosure pass.** It runs after drafting rather than during it, because both halves of it, what must appear and what must not, are easier to enforce against sentences that exist than to hold in mind while composing them.
6. **Apply the language rules.**
7. **Run the checks under "Before you return"** at the end of this file.

## The disclosure rules

Three groups. **What must appear** decides whether a fact ships. **How it is written** decides the wording of every fact that does. **When the tag and the record disagree** covers the one case that is not the writer's to resolve, and how to write the card while it stays open.

### What must appear

#### Name one or two light struggles

Every card names at least one struggle and no more than two. One keeps the card honest: a profile with nothing hard in it reads as a profile that is hiding something, and the reader stops trusting the good parts. More than two turns the card into a list of problems: one draft for a harder dog named five struggles, which took 64 of its 238 words (27%), and a reader came away with a picture of the problems rather than the dog. The model is a published card that names two struggles a reader can picture, where they happen, and that one is improving, in 35 of 233 words (15%).

Choose the two from the record:

1. **Prefer what an adopter would notice in daily life** (jumping when excited, pulling on leash, barking at the door) over what shows up only in a specific setting or under handling.
2. **Prefer struggles that come with context or progress in the record** ("mostly at the shelter," "getting better with training"), so the sentence can end on something true and hopeful.
3. **A difficulty of daily life counts toward the two wherever it appears**: being left alone, guarding, handling, jumping and mouthing, pulling on leash. HOUSEHOLD FIT's account of how the animal behaves around other dogs, cats, small animals, children, and people it does not know shows who it can live with, and does not count, even where the behavior is hard ("children frighten him"). Neither do a stated limit, unknowns ("has not been tested with cats"), or the support line. A struggle never goes in ABOUT THIS PET.

Everything else goes to the history review. Staff go over it with the adopter before the meet, where a person can give the circumstances and answer questions. Holding a struggle back makes the closing line required (see "The closing line is required" below), so the card still tells the reader that a conversation is waiting.

This is an editorial decision, not a research finding. No study measures how many struggles a card should carry.

#### One occurrence is not a pattern

A behavior recorded once is not yet a description of the animal, and publishing it describes a dog who does not exist. A dog frightened during one thunderstorm has told you about one thunderstorm. Look for recurrence before you write, and when a claim rests on a single entry that nothing else in the record echoes, leave it out.

This rule cuts anecdotes from a card. It is not an instruction to audit the card for a missing line.

#### Serious incidents go to the consultation, not onto the card

Five kinds of incident stay off the card however the record words them, and the shelter discusses them with the adopter in a consultation before they meet the animal:

1. A bite, to a person or another animal. A snap that connected is a bite.
2. An attack on another animal.
3. Guarding that made contact with a person or another animal. Contact is the line: guarding that stopped short of it (a growl, a stiffen, a snap that did not connect) is an ordinary struggle, and it competes for the card's two places like any other.
4. Anything involving a child.
5. A kill or serious injury to a cat or a small animal.

In the consultation a person can state the circumstances and the date and answer what the adopter asks back, which a web page cannot do. One entry is enough to route an incident there, and the entry does not decay with age: `references/sourcing-behavior-facts.md` covers why recency weighting never buries one.

**The incident goes; the limit it supports stays.** A child-related incident usually carries a placement limit, and that limit is true of the animal. When the incident is its only source, there is no behavior on the card to describe, so the limit publishes in HOUSEHOLD FIT as a recommendation about the home: "He is more comfortable with older teens and adults, and would do best in a home without young children." The incident behind it, and whoever reported it, does not. The same holds for a "no cats" or "only dog" limit behind an attack. When ordinary observed behavior also supports the limit, describe that behavior instead (see "Placement limits: describe the behavior behind them").

**The closing line is required when the record holds one, or when the card held back a struggle.** With nothing about the incident or the held-back struggles on the card, the closer is the only signal an adopter gets that a conversation is waiting for them:

- Most animals: end GOOD TO KNOW with "[Shelter] will review [Name]'s full history with you before you meet [him/her]."
- A Diamond Dog: end with the Diamond Dog message in `references/profile-template.md`, word for word. It carries the history-review sentence along with the program support and the way to meet the dog, so the card has no separate history line.

| Keep | Cut |
|---|---|
| "[Shelter] will review his full history with you before you meet him." | "[Shelter] will review his full history with you before you take him home." |

The cut phrasing assumes the adoption is happening and puts the disclosure at the last step. The consultation comes before the introduction, so "before you meet him" describes when the conversation actually happens.

The card's silence works only if the consultation really comes before the adopter meets the animal. If the record suggests it did not, tell the person you are writing for so they can raise it with shelter staff.

#### Placement limits: describe the behavior behind them

Limits that are true of the animal ("only dog," "no cats," "a home without young children") are real, and shelter-management systems usually publish them as structured attributes and push them to listing sites such as Petfinder and Adopt-a-Pet, where they drive the search filters. The card does a different job: it shows the behavior that set the limit, as it was seen, and lets the reader judge their own household. "When he sees a child, even across a parking lot, he tucks his tail and tries to get away" tells a family with children everything "would do best in an adult-only home" does, and it tells every other reader what the dog is like. Write it in HOUSEHOLD FIT and stop there: the description replaces the limit, it does not sit beside it, and the attribute panel already carries the filter.

State the limit itself, as a recommendation about the home ("would do best in a home without young children"), in two cases only:

1. **Its only source is a serious incident.** The incident goes to the consultation, so there is no behavior on the card to describe (see "The incident goes; the limit it supports stays").
2. **The record gives no reason for it.** A tag or a matchmaking note with no observation behind it leaves nothing to describe. Never invent the behavior.

When an ordinary observed behavior supports the limit alongside an incident, describe the behavior. This is the operator's editorial decision: no study in `references/research-basis.md` compares a described behavior with a stated limit.

The question to ask about a limit is provenance, not presence: does the record support it? See "When the filter and the record disagree" below when it does not.

**Do not spin a limit into a compliment.** The standard rewrite in shelter promotion guidance turns "must go to a home without children" into a charm sentence, on the theory that the plain version scares off even childless readers. Strip the charm from it and neither the limit nor a behavior is left: a reader can no longer say what the dog does or what household it fits.

| Keep | Cut |
|---|---|
| "Children frighten him at any age: when he sees one, even across a parking lot, he tucks his tail and tries to get away." | "He would do best in an adult-only home." |
| "In a home with cats, she locked onto one so intently that the introduction had to stop." | "She would do best in a home without cats." |
| "He is more comfortable with older teens and adults, and would do best in a home without young children." (only source: an incident held for the consultation) | "He's such a goofy bundle of energy that he might be a bit too much for small children." |

The same guidance says to lead with positives and put the challenge last. Ordering is fine; burying is not. A behavior that arrives at the tail of a sentence about how gentle the animal is has been staged so the reader discounts it.

Conditions on the *applicant* are a different thing and do not belong in a profile: "qualified adopters only," "must have a fenced yard," "experienced owners only," "serious inquiries only." Removing one of those costs no adopter a good match, because it says nothing about the animal. That is the mechanism: an elimination question shrinks the applicant pool while telling nobody anything about the dog.

#### A narrowed yes names its limit

When the record pairs a broad "good with" attribute with an attribute that narrows it, the copy shows the limit as well as the yes: the behavior behind the narrowing when the record has it, and the limit itself when the record gives nothing but the tag (the second case under "Placement limits: describe the behavior behind them"). A bare "good with kids" on a dog that also carries "No Small Kids (10 or under)" tells a family with a six-year-old that the dog suits them, when the record says it does not.

Shelter-management systems name these attributes differently. Pairs like these are the ones to look for:

| Broad attribute | Narrowing attribute | The limit the copy names |
|---|---|---|
| Good with Kids | No Small Kids (10 or under) | The children's age: a home without children 10 and under |
| Good with Dogs | Dog Selective | The kinds of dogs the record shows the dog has done well with, as a recommendation about the home |
| Good with Dogs | No Small Dogs | The size: a home without small dogs |

An attribute that records a meeting, such as "Has Met Kids Over 10 and Done Well," is not a limit and does not narrow the broad one. When you are unsure whether one of your system's attributes narrows another, ask.

| Keep | Cut |
|---|---|
| "He has done well with older kids he has met, and would do best in a home without children 10 and under." | "He is good with kids." |

For Dog Selective, name the dogs, not a handling plan: "has played well with larger dogs at the shelter" describes the dog, while "does best with slow introductions" tells the adopter what to do and is cut under "Disclose behavior, never prescribe handling" below. Introductions may appear only as an observation of what happened. When the record names no kind of dog the dog has done well with, flag it for staff and leave the kind out rather than inventing one.

The narrowing attribute is the default, like any tag. When the record contradicts it (the dog has done well with children under 10, or also carries an attribute recording that it met young children and did well), follow "When the filter and the record disagree, write what was seen and flag it" below. Never drop the limit, and never write the broad yes alone.

Neither the limit line nor a HOUSEHOLD FIT description of how the dog does with other dogs counts toward the card's one or two struggles (item 3 under "Name one or two light struggles").

### How it is written

#### Plain words, not clinical ones

Write the behavior the way a person without training would say it, at about an eighth-grade reading level (Humane World for Animals, 2025). "Resource guarding," "reactivity," "redirection," "barrier frustration," and "arousal" are the shelter's working vocabulary and mean little to the public. Some of them sound more alarming than the behavior is; others sound like nothing at all.

| The record's words | What gets published |
|---|---|
| Jumpy and mouthy in kennel | Can get jumpy when he is excited. The jumping mostly happens at the shelter, and it is getting better with training. |
| Leash reactive to dogs | Barks and pulls toward other dogs he passes on walks. |
| Stranger-reactive at the kennel door | Takes a little time to warm up to new people: he barks when someone new comes to his door, and settles once they give him time and space. |
| Resource guards food and high-value treats | Likes to eat in peace, and may growl if someone reaches toward his bowl. |

Shelter place and routine names (kennel, run, play yard, playgroup, quiet room, intake, and the shelter's own location names) are shelter vocabulary too, and readers outside the shelter do not use them (Humane World for Animals, 2025, p. 19, "Avoid shelter jargon"). The card still names where each behavior was seen, in adopter words, because the rule that a card gives an observation and not a prediction depends on the setting.

| The record's words | What gets published |
|---|---|
| in playgroup, shelter playgroups | in a group of dogs, played in groups with other dogs |
| the play yards | a fenced yard |
| comes out of her kennel | as the leash goes on, on the way out for a walk |
| in his kennel, at the kennel | at the shelter |
| kennel door | when someone comes to her door |
| kennel fence, run fence | through a fence at the shelter |
| quiet room | a room with a couch |
| intake, at intake | when she arrived, her first weeks at the shelter |
| [the shelter's own location names] | the plain description of the place |

**Phrase each struggle as positively as the facts allow.** Show it as a scene, and add what the record says about where it happens, what has been seen to help, or how it is changing. A gentle opening clause ("takes a little time to warm up") tells the reader how to hold the information; the example after it tells them what it looks like. Each positive clause is itself an observation with a source. It is never a prescription for the adopter ("needs a home that...") or a promise ("he will grow out of it").

**Write a struggle as a behavior, never as a trait.** "Timid," "sensitive," "dominant," "hyperactive," "reactive," "anxious," and "stubborn" name a kind of dog; the behavior names a moment a reader can picture and judge for their own home. Across 70,733 listings, negative trait words went with the longest stays of any wording tested: "dominant" 1.46 times as long, "sensitive" 1.42, "hyperactive" 1.32, "timid" 1.20 (Nakamura et al., 2019). "Takes a little time to warm up: he barks when someone new comes to his door, and settles once they give him space" carries the same fact as "timid" without the label.

**The behavior stays recognizable.** After the gentle framing, a reader should still be able to say what the dog does. "Takes a little time to warm up: he barks when someone new comes to his door" passes. "Is picky about his friends" names no behavior a reader can picture, and fails.

#### Patterns, not moments

Every behavior statement describes how the animal generally is. None retells one occasion.

An observation log records moments; a profile publishes the pattern behind them. "She will chase a flying disc for forty minutes" is the pattern; "one volunteer had to end a disc session after fifty minutes" is the moment that demonstrated it, and the moment does not ship. This binds favorable copy exactly as it binds disclosures.

**Publish no incident specifics.** No dates, no handler or volunteer, no what-preceded-it, no body-language detail such as a lip curl or a startle.

**Never attribute a behavior to a previous owner or family.** Their reports are private information, and a line like "his previous family reported incidents with young children" publishes both an incident and its source. The pattern survives the translation; the source and the occasion do not: "He is more comfortable with older teens and adults, and would do best in a home without young children."

The full record stays in the foster file and the behavior logs.

**Say why the dog is at the shelter when it was no fault of the dog's.** Many shelter dogs arrive through circumstances that had nothing to do with them: an owner who moved, a landlord who would not allow dogs, an owner's illness or death, arriving as a stray, a transfer from a crowded shelter. Readers ask for this (Kelling et al., 2026), and a reader who is not told fills the gap with a guess that usually blames the dog. Where the record carries a reason like these, state it plainly in one clause in ABOUT THIS PET: "[Name] came to [Shelter] when his family moved somewhere that did not allow dogs." Where the reason is an incident, a conflict, or anything that would identify or characterize the surrendering family, the rule above governs and it does not ship; the card then says nothing about it.

What is never written is the softened middle. "Her last owners could not meet all her needs" reads as a fault in the dog where "her last owners surrendered her" reads as a circumstance. Say the plain thing or say nothing.

#### Disclose the observation, not the prediction

A behavior seen at the shelter or in foster is a fact about that setting. Writing it as a property the animal carries into the reader's home is a prediction, and the prediction is usually wrong. In the study behind this rule, a shelter assessment of resource guarding was right 47.4% of the time: more than half of the dogs flagged as guarders by staff or by their surrendering owner did not guard in their adoptive homes. The behavior is worth publishing, and the forecast built on it is not.

| Keep | Cut |
|---|---|
| "He guards his bowl at the shelter and may growl if someone reaches toward it" | "He will guard food from you" |
| "He has gone after another dog through a fence at the shelter" | "He will always need an experienced handler" |
| "He barks at visitors and takes time to warm up" | "He will never be comfortable with strangers" |

This is the mirror of the promise rule in the language section. "Great with all dogs" fails because it promises a good outcome the record cannot support; "will always need management" fails the same way, in the same sentence position, about a bad one.

#### Disclose behavior, never prescribe handling

A profile states what the animal does. It does not tell the adopter how to handle it. The adoption team delivers training guidance, management plans, and handling protocols during the consult, where it can tailor them to the actual home. A web page cannot, and a management plan read cold reads as a list of demands on the adopter.

| Keep | Cut |
|---|---|
| "He guards his bowl at the shelter and may growl if someone reaches toward it" | "so he needs a feeding station he can be called away from" |
| "On leash he barks and pulls toward other dogs" | "which is easier to manage when he wears a harness" |
| "She jumps up on people when she is excited" | "so turn away until all four paws are on the floor" |
| "He barks at visitors and takes time to warm up" | "so he needs a space of his own when guests are over" |

After drafting, reread every "so...", "he needs...", and "he does best with..." clause. Each one is prescription unless it states a limit under one of the two cases in "Placement limits: describe the behavior behind them."

Name the program the animal is enrolled in (such as the Diamond Dog Program), and list the cues it knows. Both describe the animal rather than instruct the adopter, so both stay.

**The failure to watch for is the compound one: a disclosure with an elimination clause attached to it.** Two rules already forbid each half, "Placement limits: describe the behavior behind them" for the screen and this section for the instruction, but they arrive together and in one breath because a writer who has just published something alarming reaches for a sentence that makes it safe. A typical example: "She has bitten when her boundaries weren't respected. For this reason, she is looking for a confident, experienced pet parent who uses positive reinforcement." The bite belongs in the consultation rather than on the card, and everything after "for this reason" is an elimination question, on a senior dog whose reader pool needs widening rather than narrowing. Publish the behavior that belongs on the card, and stop. The reader decides whether they are the right home; the card does not decide it for them.

### When the tag and the record disagree

#### When the filter and the record disagree, write what was seen and flag it

The tags in the shelter-management system are set by staff who have met the animal, and they are the operating default: write copy consistent with the tag. But a tag that contradicts the written observations is worth catching, because it may be stale, and because a wrong tag silently removes the animal from every search that filters on it.

When a tag and the record disagree, or when the record contradicts itself on a constraint, **write the card from what was observed, and flag the conflict; do not resolve it in the prose.** On the point the tag covers, the card reports only what someone saw ("in her foster home she sniffed noses with the family cat and has kept away from it since") and makes no claim either way: it neither repeats the tag nor turns the observation into a limit the record does not set. Write the rest of the card as usual, then, alongside the draft, name the tag, the entries, and how they conflict, so the person you are writing for can take it to shelter staff. Never flip a tag's meaning on your own reading of the file, and never quietly drop a constraint because the notes look thin.

The flag is part of the deliverable, because a profile does not publish while its prose and its attribute panel disagree. The conflict clears one of two ways, and both need staff: either the tag is wrong and staff update it, leaving the prose to stand, or the tag is right and the observation behind the prose was partial, so the prose changes to agree with the tag.

## Language rules

The research finding these rest on is counterintuitive: emotional, anthropomorphizing copy underperforms factual copy (Markowitz, 2020). `references/research-basis.md` carries the study. That finding is about emotional appeals and social words, not about warmth: a card can be positive, lively, and creative as long as each sentence rests on something the dog does. When instinct and these rules conflict, follow the rules.

- **Factual, not emotional.** Every sentence carries something a reader could check. "Look at the cuteness! This boy is adorable and full of love" is the register to avoid.
- **Third person, never the animal speaking.** Write about the animal ("Tiger came to the shelter as a stray"), not as the animal ("Hi, I'm Tiger!"). Among 4,460 rehomed cats, profiles written in the first person went with stays about two and a half days longer than third-person ones, age and sex controlled (Rix et al., 2021), and a first-person card makes an unobserved fact read as an evasion. The checker fails any first-person pronoun. Addressing the reader as "you" is a different voice and is encouraged (see "Build the picture" below): the animal never speaks, but the reader can step into the scene.
- **Positive and creative, built on behavior.** Lead with what the dog does well, and give it energy: vivid verbs, a specific setting, a nickname or a light line that rests on an observed behavior ("the king of fetch," "will roll over for belly rubs the first chance he gets"). The test is the source, not the tone: a playful line about a real behavior passes, and a warm line with no behavior behind it ("full of love") does not.
- **Show what being with the animal is like.** Tell the observations from the side of someone spending time with the dog: shared moments with people or other dogs, contact, play, routines, and what happened next. "She stretches out on the couch beside whoever is reading and rests her chin on their knee. She leans into a hand that scratches her ears and rolls onto her back for belly rubs as soon as someone sits on the floor" lets a reader picture an afternoon with her. "She knows sit, down, wait, and touch, and she is learning stay. She sits the moment she is asked and works eagerly for treats." is accurate and reads as an evaluator's log of how she performs. Both are observations; the first is the one to aim for. The card is a narrative that answers "what would it be like to live with this animal." When the record has foster-home or outing observations (car rides, events, time alone, crate, household routines), lead with them (Humane World for Animals, 2025, p. 24); for example: "In her foster home she rides quietly in the car and settles in her crate while the family is out." Most records have none. The card then tells the shelter observations in adopter words as life with the dog and never invents a home scene. GOOD TO KNOW follows the same register: name each struggle so a reader can picture it, then end on where it happens or how it is improving. A scene uses only what the record holds: never add a detail to make a sentence flow, never join two separate observations into one scene, and never write an inner state ("he dreams of," "he knows he is home") that nobody observed.
- **Build the picture, one moment leading to the next.** The card sells a life with the animal: a reader should finish it able to picture an ordinary day together and wanting it. A section does that when its sentences build on each other instead of standing side by side. The moment is a typical one, built from what the record shows again and again (see "Patterns, not moments"): "on walks she wants to see everything," not "on one walk she asked for another loop." Move through it: where it happens, what the animal does, what happens next, with time and sequence words ("when," "then," "once," "after," "by the end of the walk") carrying the reader from one observation to the one it really followed. Put the reader in the moment with "you" when the record supports it ("Sit with her on a bench and she leans her whole body into you"). Use strong, specific verbs ("hauls on a tug rope," "noses through the grass") over flat ones ("plays," "sniffs around"), and keep a source's own vivid phrase when it is apt ("a dog's dog"). Being creative means choosing, framing, and ordering what the record holds; it never means adding to it, and the sourcing limits in the rule above still bind every sentence.

  | Reads as a list | Builds the picture |
  |---|---|
  | [Name] makes little grunts as she sniffs around on a walk. In the yard she runs zoomies, chases a thrown ball, and pulls hard at tug of war. She leans in for scratches on her ears, back, and belly. | Take [Name] for a walk and you'll hear her soon enough: she makes little piglet grunts as she noses through the grass. In a fenced yard she tears around in zoomies and hauls on a tug rope with everything she has. Sit with her on a bench afterward and she leans her whole body into you for ear and belly scratches. |
  | [Name] knows sit, touch, leave it, and find it, and she looks up at her name. When her walker stops, she comes and sits in front of them. | [Name] knows sit, touch, leave it, and find it. Stop on a walk and look at her, and she comes and sits right in front of you. |

  The left column is accurate, passes the checker, and reads as an inventory; every sentence could be moved without loss. The right column holds the same facts and nothing more.

  Joining unrelated facts with "and" is not a connection: it is the same list packed into one sentence. "Toss a ball for him and he brings it back, and he will work hard for a treat" puts two behaviors side by side that never happened together. Connect only what shares a real moment or setting in the record. A fact with no moment to join gets its own sentence where it fits best, or stays off the card if it is not required.

  Keep the details an adopter would notice and leave out the ones an evaluator records. Behavior notes read a dog's body language ("soft eyes," "mouth open," "loose body," "tail at mid-height") to judge whether it is relaxed; on a card those words describe nothing a reader cares about. Write what the dog did ("leans into you while you pet him," "walks right up to you"), not the signs a trainer used to read it.

  When the notes keep returning to one trait, you may build the card around it: a dog whose walkers keep writing about how much he loves the trails can carry ABOUT THIS PET from the trail, to the games he plays outside, to the bench where he settles beside you. This is an option, not a requirement, and only for a trait the record actually repeats. Build it from the trait's good side; a struggle that comes with it (balking at the door on the way back in) goes to GOOD TO KNOW or the history review.
- **Cut platitudes and filler.** "Forever home," "so much love to give," "heart of gold," "won't last long." Test each phrase by deleting it: if no fact or setting goes with it, it was filler. `scripts/check_draft.py` lists the common ones.
- **Concrete behavior over personality labels, positive ones included.** Describe what the animal does, not what it is like. In 70,733 listings, the kind words went with longer stays too: "friendly" 1.14 times as long, "gentle" 1.14, "quiet" 1.15, "obedient" 1.24, "trainable" 1.25 (Nakamura et al., 2019). "Protective," "loyal," and "affectionate" are labels a reader cannot check, and so are inner states: "calm," "at ease," "content," "happy." Write the behavior that showed the trait ("leans against you and rolls over for belly rubs," "lies down in the sun once outside"). A positive word may head that behavior ("calm: he sleeps through the barking around him and settles on his bed within minutes of coming in"), since readers rated a word-plus-behavior description above no text at all (Archer et al., 2025); it may not stand in for the behavior, which is the form the listings data penalizes.
- **Show energy as activity.** "Energetic" and "active" went with longer stays (1.22 and 1.16 times as long); "lively" and "eager" went with shorter ones (0.88 and 0.92). Write what the dog does, for how long, and how it looks doing it: "fetches for over an hour without slowing down," "wrestles and chases with other dogs." An upbeat word belongs beside that behavior ("lively with other dogs, wrestling and chasing"), not in place of it. "Energetic" is the one word both studies agree on: it was also the only description readers rated below no text at all (Archer et al., 2025), so it stays out in any position.
- **Never name a trait to deny it.** "Not aggressive," "no resource guarding," and "doesn't have a mean bone" plant the question they answer, and listings that said "not dominant" or "not hyperactive" ran long. Say what the dog does instead, or say nothing.
- **Record gaps go to staff, not into the copy.** "The ages are not recorded" and "his listing marks this as to be determined" beside a tag read as contradicting the tag. State what the tag and the record say, and put the gap in your open items.
- **Plain vocabulary over shelter vocabulary.** See the tables above.
- **Open on identity, not statistics.** The first sentence says who the animal is. "You haven't lived until you've had Sammy's nose on your lap" beats "Sammy is a 5-year-old shepherd mix with hip dysplasia."
- **Keep the breed out of the copy.** The attribute panel shows it, and the label itself costs interest: readers rated the same photos lower with a breed label than without, and three shelters that took breed labels off their cards cut median length of stay from 30 to 19 days (Archer et al., 2025, and Cohen et al., 2020 cited there). "Hound mix" in the first sentence or anywhere after it comes out; the checker lists breed words.
- **Drop social and humanizing words.** "Buddy," "friend," "helper," "sweetheart," "best friend," "companion," and the pet-as-person nicknames: "lady," "gentleman," "angel," "princess," "old soul," "shadow," "velcro dog," "love bug," "fur baby." These correlate with animals that did not get adopted.
- **Drop superlatives and absolute claims.** "Extremely," "adorable," "the perfect," "100%," "loves everyone," "always friendly," "never aggressive." The absolutes promise an outcome the record cannot back.
- **Drop scolding and gatekeeping closers.** "Qualified adopters only," "serious inquiries only," "no apartments," "must apply." For a Diamond Dog the Diamond Dog message is the closer; otherwise the history-review line is, when the record needs one. Encourage a response rather than screening for one.
- **No em-dashes.** Use colons, parentheses, or commas.

## When rules pull against each other

The register rules and the picture come first, together: a factual voice, observable behavior instead of personality labels, and observations instead of promises, told as moments that build into a picture of life with the animal. A card correct in register that reads as a list of attributes is not finished; it is the most common reason a draft that passes the checker is sent back. Keeping the card inside 175-250 words comes next, then covering all seven topics, then keeping out social words and conditions on the adopter. Never trade a covered topic for length: cut filler, platitudes, and repetition first. When a card runs long or reads as mostly problems, a third struggle is the first thing to move to the history review.

Three defects stop a profile from shipping whatever else it does well: no struggle named anywhere, prose that contradicts a tag, and an absolute claim. A broad yes with no limit beside a narrowing tag is prose that contradicts a tag; the writer adds the limit from the tag, with no need to flag it. A card that reports an observation a tag disagrees with is still written and returned, with the conflict flagged for staff, as "When the filter and the record disagree" describes; it waits on staff before it is published.

## Length and photos

Target 175 to 250 words across the four sections: every word under the four headings, including the support sentence and the closing lines of GOOD TO KNOW. Image and link markup does not count, and neither do program boilerplate blocks (an adoption-fee block, a senior program block), which describe the shelter's offer rather than the animal. The Diamond Dog message is about 55 of those words, which leaves about 195 for the rest.

Never buy a shorter card by dropping the card's one or two struggles, and do not pad to reach the floor. A profile that lands under 175 is usually one that skipped a topic rather than one that wrote tightly, so check coverage before adding words. A card within 20% of the band (140 to 300 words) is a small miss; past that, it reads as a different kind of card, and the checker reports which side of the line a draft is on. Most long cards get there through filler and repeated facts, which is where to cut.

Target three to five photos on the profile itself. One or two is too few to reduce an adopter's uncertainty, and that is where most profiles sit. Adding a second and third photo is usually the cheapest improvement left in a finished profile.

Do not count automatically generated galleries, such as a feed of volunteer walk photos appended to every profile. They fill up on their own and usually do not syndicate to listing sites, so a full gallery is not a substitute for putting photos on the profile.

## Before you return

Run these against the finished draft. Each one restates a rule above, gathered here because this is where they fire.

1. **Count the struggles on the whole card.** One or two, each one a behavior a reader can picture, and the struggle sentences under 20% of the narrative words (the support sentence and closing lines do not count toward that share). A third struggle moves to the history review.
2. **Reread every "so...", "he needs...", and "he does best with..." clause.** Each is prescription unless it states a limit under one of the two cases in "Placement limits."
3. **Check the record once more for the five serious incidents.** Confirm none reached the card in any wording, that any placement limit behind one is stated in HOUSEHOLD FIT, and that the closing line (or the Diamond Dog message) is present whenever an incident or a held-back struggle needs it.
4. **Confirm all four headings exist**, GOOD TO KNOW included, and that the content covers all seven topics: About, Likes, Dogs, Cats, Kids, Training, Struggles. "Untested" (dogs, cats) or "not known" (children) covers a topic. Then check the record for a no-fault reason the dog is at the shelter, and state it if the card does not.
5. **Read the prose against the attribute panel.** If they disagree, keep the card on that point to what was observed, with no claim either way, save the draft, and flag the conflict alongside it for staff: the tag, the entries, and how they conflict. Do not stop without a draft. One exception needs no flag: when the panel shows a narrowed pair under "A narrowed yes names its limit" and the prose gives the broad yes alone, add the limit from the tag. Flag only when the record contradicts the narrowing tag.
6. **Read the first sentence alone.** It names the animal and says something it does, not its breed, age, or medical status.
7. **Read each section for a scene, then for its sources.** Read it aloud. If its sentences could be put in any order without loss, it is a list: rework it under "Build the picture" so each sentence leads to the next, and check that a reader could picture a moment with the animal, not just its attributes. Then trace every detail in a connected sentence to the record or to the person you are writing for: a scene that reads well because a detail was added or two observations were joined has invented something.
8. **Reread each setting on the card for shelter place and routine names**, including the shelter's own location names, which the checker cannot catch, and rewrite them with the second table under "Plain words, not clinical ones."
9. **Run the checker on the draft.** Save the draft as markdown with the four headings, then run `python3 scripts/check_draft.py <draft.md>` from this skill's folder (standard library only). It prints PASS or the list to fix: missing headings, word count outside 175 to 250 (and whether it is more than 20% outside), social words, screening phrases, absolute claims, platitudes, em-dashes, struggle sentences over 20% of the card, negative trait words and denials (and the positive labels, listed for review), shelter-setting words (listed for review), first-person pronouns, breed words, a support sentence sharing a line with other copy, and a missing or reworded Diamond Dog message. It also reports a page address above ABOUT THIS PET when the draft has one. Fix everything it lists and run it again. If Python is not available, do the same checks by hand against the lists in "Language rules" and say that the script did not run.
