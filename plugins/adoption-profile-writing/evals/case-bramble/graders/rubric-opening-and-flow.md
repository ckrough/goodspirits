Grade the profile in profile.md. If the file is missing or empty, FAIL. Grade only the text under the four headings, and skip the program blocks in GOOD TO KNOW (a senior program, the Diamond Dog message, the history-review line).

Rubric dimensions 6 and 10, identity-forward opening and narrative flow. The card should sell a life with the animal: a reader finishes it able to picture ordinary days together. It reads as fluid, natural prose written for this one animal, not as a list of facts.

What each section is for:
- ABOUT THIS PET builds the picture. It moves through typical moments with the animal, drawn from what the animal does again and again, and something in each sentence puts the reader there: the reader as "you", what brings the behavior out ("Bring out a ball and", "Once he warms up to someone"), where or when it happens, or what comes next.
- HOUSEHOLD FIT goes topic by topic through who the animal would live with: other dogs, cats, small animals, children, people it does not know. That order is its structure, not a list, as long as each topic says what the animal did or does ("she locked onto one so intently that the introduction had to stop") rather than giving a bare verdict ("No cats. Good with dogs.").
- TRAINING names what the animal knows and how it works with a person. It may be short.

FAIL when any of these holds:
1. Opening. The first sentence under ABOUT THIS PET opens on breed, age, size, medical status, or a generic adjective ("Meet this sweet senior"), not on who the animal is or something it does.
2. Inventory. ABOUT THIS PET is a run of standalone attributes: bare statements or comma-delimited facts with nothing that puts the reader with the animal. "She makes little grunts as she sniffs around on a walk. In the yard she runs zoomies, chases a thrown ball, and pulls hard at tug of war. She leans in for scratches on her ears, back, and belly." is an inventory. The same facts as a picture: "Take her for a walk and you'll hear her soon enough: she makes little piglet grunts as she noses through the grass. In a fenced yard she tears around in zoomies and hauls on a tug rope with everything she has. Sit with her on a bench afterward and she leans her whole body into you for ear and belly scratches."
3. Unrelated facts joined. A sentence anywhere joins two behaviors that have nothing to do with each other ("He chases a ball down and brings it back for another throw, and he will work hard for a treat").
4. One moment made a trait. A sentence builds a trait on a single occasion ("He never wants a walk to end: after one long loop he asked for another"). An occasion reported plainly as something that happened ("she has dozed off on a dog bed beside live music") is fine.
5. Evaluator's or coined wording. The card uses body-language readings or phrases an adopter would not say ("leans in with his mouth open", "soft eyes", "loose body", "on a bench break").
6. Bare verdicts. HOUSEHOLD FIT gives verdicts without behavior for most of its topics.
7. Embellishment. An invented inner state ("he dreams of", "he knows he is home"), a detail no one could have observed, or filler that carries no fact.

PASS when none holds. FAIL otherwise, giving the number of each condition met and the words that met it.
